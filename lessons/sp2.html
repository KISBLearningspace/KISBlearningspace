Range, Quartiles, Interquartile Range and Percentiles

Guiding questions

  • Why is the average not enough to describe a data set?
  • How do the quartiles split an ordered data set into four equal parts?
  • How do we decide whether a value is an outlier?

Learning outcomes

  • Find the range of a data set and explain what it tells us.
  • Find the median, lower quartile Q₁ and upper quartile Q₃ for small data sets.
  • Calculate the interquartile range (IQR) and explain why it is more reliable than the range.
  • Use the 1.5 × IQR rule to test for outliers.
  • Describe a percentile and find simple percentiles.

2Study Notes

Measures of spread tell us how far apart the data values are. Two data sets can have the same mean but very different spread.
Range = largest value − smallest value. It is easy to find but one extreme value changes it a lot.
Quartiles. Put the data in order. The median (Q₂) splits the data into two halves. Q₁ (lower quartile) is the median of the lower half. Q₃ (upper quartile) is the median of the upper half. If n is odd, leave the middle value out of both halves.
Interquartile range IQR = Q₃ − Q₁. It is the spread of the middle 50% of the data, so it is not affected by extreme values.
Outliers. A value is an outlier if it is less than Q₁ − 1.5 × IQR or greater than Q₃ + 1.5 × IQR.
Percentiles. The k-th percentile is the value below which k% of the data lie. The median is the 50th percentile, Q₁ is the 25th and Q₃ is the 75th. For a small data set, position = ⟦k|100⟧ × n. If it is not a whole number, round up and take that value. If it is whole, take the mean of that value and the next one. (Calculators and software use slightly different rules, so answers may differ by a little.)

Example 1 (range and median). Data: 8, 3, 15, 6, 12, 5, 9. Find the range and the median.
  1. Put the data in order. 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 15.
  2. Range = largest − smallest. 15 − 3 = 12.
  3. n = 7 is odd. The median is the 4th value. 8.
Answer: Range = 12; median = 8.
Example 2 (quartiles, n odd). Find Q₁, Q₃ and the IQR for 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 15.
  1. The median is 8 (the 4th value). Leave it out of both halves. Lower half: 3, 5, 6. Upper half: 9, 12, 15.
  2. Q₁ is the median of the lower half. Q₁ = 5.
  3. Q₃ is the median of the upper half. Q₃ = 12.
  4. IQR = Q₃ − Q₁. 12 − 5 = 7.
Answer: Q₁ = 5, Q₃ = 12, IQR = 7.
Example 3 (quartiles, n even). Find the median, Q₁, Q₃ and IQR for 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 15.
  1. n = 8. The median is the mean of the 4th and 5th values. (7 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7.5.
  2. Split into two halves of 4. Lower: 2, 4, 5, 7. Upper: 8, 10, 11, 15.
  3. Q₁ is the mean of the middle two of the lower half. (4 + 5) ÷ 2 = 4.5.
  4. Q₃ is the mean of the middle two of the upper half. (10 + 11) ÷ 2 = 10.5.
  5. IQR. 10.5 − 4.5 = 6.
Answer: Median 7.5, Q₁ = 4.5, Q₃ = 10.5, IQR = 6.
Example 4 (same mean, different spread). Class A scores: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52. Class B scores: 20, 35, 50, 65, 80. Compare the ranges and IQRs.
  1. Both have the median 50 and the mean 50. The centre is the same.
  2. Ranges. A: 52 − 48 = 4. B: 80 − 20 = 60.
  3. IQRs: Q₁ and Q₃ are the medians of the halves. A: Q₁ = 48.5, Q₃ = 51.5, IQR = 3. B: Q₁ = 27.5, Q₃ = 72.5, IQR = 45.
  4. Compare. Class B is much more spread out. Class A results are very consistent.
Answer: Class A: range 4, IQR 3. Class B: range 60, IQR 45.
Example 5 (test for an outlier). Data: 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 30. Is 30 an outlier?
  1. Find the quartiles. n = 8, halves of 4. Lower: 4, 6, 7, 8 so Q₁ = 6.5. Upper: 9, 10, 12, 30 so Q₃ = 11.
  2. IQR. 11 − 6.5 = 4.5.
  3. Upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5 × IQR. 11 + 1.5 × 4.5 = 11 + 6.75 = 17.75.
  4. Compare 30 with the fence. 30 > 17.75, so 30 is an outlier.
  5. Lower fence for a check. 6.5 − 6.75 = −0.25. No values are below this.
Answer: Yes, 30 is an outlier (above 17.75).
Example 6 (range is affected, IQR is not). Remove the outlier 30 from Example 5 and find the range and IQR of 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12. Compare with the original.
  1. New range. 12 − 4 = 8 (before it was 30 − 4 = 26).
  2. New quartiles: median 8; lower 4, 6, 7 gives Q₁ = 6; upper 9, 10, 12 gives Q₃ = 10. IQR = 10 − 6 = 4 (before it was 4.5).
  3. Compare the changes. The range changed from 26 to 8 but the IQR only changed from 4.5 to 4.
Answer: The IQR is much more stable than the range.
Example 7 (a percentile). There are 20 test scores in order. Which score is the 90th percentile?
  1. Find the position. ⟦90|100⟧ × 20 = 18.
  2. The position is a whole number, so take the mean of the 18th and 19th scores. Look at the ordered list and average them.
  3. Interpret. About 90% of the students scored at or below this value.
Answer: The mean of the 18th and 19th scores.
Example 8 (using the quartiles). For a data set n = 40, Q₁ = 12 and Q₃ = 30. About how many values are between 12 and 30?
  1. Q₁ to Q₃ contains the middle 50%. 0.5 × 40.
  2. Calculate. 20.
Answer: About 20 values.
Common mistakes.
① Not putting the data in order first.
② Including the median in both halves when n is odd (leave it out).
③ Writing the quartiles as “Q₃ − Q₁” without calculating, or the IQR as an interval.
④ Using the range with outliers present: the IQR is safer.
⑤ Calling a value an outlier without calculating the fences.

3History & Facts

  • Francis Galton (1822–1911): introduced percentiles and the “quartile deviation” while measuring human characteristics.
  • John Tukey (1915–2000): invented the box plot and the 1.5 × IQR outlier rule, now used worldwide.
  • Percentiles today: used in growth charts, standardised exams and salary reports.

5Real-World Applications

  • Salaries: reports often give the median and IQR because a few very high salaries are outliers.
  • Test scores: “the middle 50% of students scored between 58 and 76”.
  • Weather: daily temperature spread in a city.
  • Sport: consistency of a player’s scores.
  • Health: blood pressure readings that are far from the rest.

7Classwork & Homework

Classwork

  1. Find the range and median of 14, 9, 11, 20, 6, 13, 17.
    AnswerOrdered: 6, 9, 11, 13, 14, 17, 20. Range = 14, median = 13.
  2. Find Q₁ and Q₃ for 6, 9, 11, 13, 14, 17, 20.
    AnswerQ₁ = 9, Q₃ = 17.
  3. Find the IQR for 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 15.
    AnswerQ₁ = 4.5, Q₃ = 10.5, IQR = 6.
  4. Q₁ = 20, Q₃ = 32. State the outlier fences.
    AnswerIQR = 12; lower 20 − 18 = 2, upper 32 + 18 = 50.

Homework

  1. Find the five-number summary of 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 40.
    AnswerMin 10; Q₁ = (12 + 13) ÷ 2 = 12.5; median 16; Q₃ = (19 + 21) ÷ 2 = 20; max 40.
  2. Using the data above, is 40 an outlier?
    AnswerIQR = 7.5; upper fence 20 + 11.25 = 31.25; 40 > 31.25, so yes.
  3. Class A has IQR 5 and class B has IQR 14. What does this mean?
    AnswerClass B results are more spread out; class A are more consistent.
  4. For n = 60, how many values lie between Q₁ and Q₃?
    AnswerAbout 30.

Paper 2 style

Question 4 [7 marks] The times (seconds) for 11 runners: 52, 55, 56, 58, 59, 60, 61, 63, 64, 66, 90. (a) Find the median, Q₁, Q₃ and IQR. [4] (b) Show that 90 is an outlier. [2] (c) Give one reason why the IQR is better than the range here. [1]
