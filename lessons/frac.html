Fractions: Add, Subtract, Multiply and Divide

KISB Diploma Programme · Mathematics: analysis and approaches · Foundation skills · start here · notes · lab · practice · exam-style questions · IA ideas

1IB Syllabus Link & Learning Outcomes

Syllabus: Prior learning for IB Mathematics: analysis and approaches (number and algebra, SL 1.x). Every topic from algebra to calculus uses fractions, so this lesson starts from the very beginning and builds up in small steps. No calculator is needed.

These are revision sections for older students, so use this lesson first and then try those questions.

Guiding questions

  • What does a fraction really mean?
  • Why can we not add fractions by adding the bottom numbers?
  • Why does dividing by a fraction give a bigger answer?

Learning outcomes

  • Name the parts of a fraction and write equivalent fractions and simplest form.
  • Add and subtract fractions with the same and with different denominators, including mixed numbers.
  • Multiply fractions, including finding a fraction of an amount and using cancelling.
  • Divide by a fraction by multiplying by its reciprocal, and check answers with a picture.

2Study Notes

Fraction. A fraction shows equal parts of a whole. In ⟦3|4⟧ the denominator (bottom, 4) says how many equal parts the whole is split into. The numerator (top, 3) says how many of those parts we take. So ⟦3|4⟧ means 3 parts out of 4.
Kinds of fractions.
• Proper fraction: numerator smaller than denominator, e.g. ⟦2|5⟧ (less than 1).
• Improper fraction: numerator bigger than (or equal to) the denominator, e.g. ⟦7|4⟧ (1 or more).
• Mixed number: a whole number and a fraction, e.g. 1⟦3|4⟧ = 1 + ⟦3|4⟧ = ⟦7|4⟧.
Equivalent fractions. Fractions that show the same amount, e.g. ⟦1|2⟧ = ⟦2|4⟧ = ⟦3|6⟧. We get them by multiplying (or dividing) the top and bottom by the same number. To write a fraction in simplest form, divide the top and bottom by their highest common factor (HCF) until no number divides both.
Common denominator. To add or subtract we need the same denominator. Use the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the denominators. For example the LCM of 6 and 8 is 24 (the smallest number in both the 6 times table and the 8 times table).
Reciprocal. Turn a fraction upside down: the reciprocal of ⟦2|3⟧ is ⟦3|2⟧. The reciprocal of 5 (= ⟦5|1⟧) is ⟦1|5⟧.
The four rules, in short
① Add / subtract: same denominator first, then add or subtract the tops. The denominator stays the same.
② Multiply: top × top, bottom × bottom.
③ Divide: keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, flip the second fraction (“keep, change, flip”).
④ Always simplify the final answer, and turn improper fractions into mixed numbers if asked.
Example 1 (read a fraction). A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices. Ahmed eats 3 slices. What fraction did he eat, and what fraction is left?
  1. Find the denominator. The whole pizza is split into 8 equal parts, so the bottom number is 8.
  2. Find the numerator. Ahmed ate 3 parts, so the top number is 3.
  3. Write the fraction eaten. ⟦3|8⟧.
  4. Find the fraction left. 8 − 3 = 5 slices are left, so ⟦5|8⟧ is left. Check: ⟦3|8⟧ + ⟦5|8⟧ = ⟦8|8⟧ = 1 whole ✓.
Answer: Eaten ⟦3|8⟧; left ⟦5|8⟧.
Example 2 (equivalent fractions and simplest form). (a) Find the missing number: ⟦2|3⟧ = ⟦?|12⟧. (b) Write ⟦18|24⟧ in simplest form.
  1. (a) Compare the denominators. 3 became 12, so we multiplied by 4 (12 ÷ 3 = 4).
  2. (a) Do the same to the numerator. 2 × 4 = 8. So ⟦2|3⟧ = ⟦8|12⟧.
  3. (b) Find the HCF of 18 and 24. Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. The highest common factor is 6.
  4. (b) Divide top and bottom by 6. 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4.
  5. (b) Check that nothing else divides both 3 and 4. No. So ⟦18|24⟧ = ⟦3|4⟧.
Answer: (a) 8; (b) ⟦3|4⟧.
Example 3 (add fractions with the same denominator). Work out ⟦2|7⟧ + ⟦3|7⟧.
  1. Check the denominators. Both are 7, so the parts are the same size.
  2. Add the numerators and keep the denominator. 2 + 3 = 5, so the answer is ⟦5|7⟧.
  3. Do not add the denominators. The size of the pieces (sevenths) does not change when we count them.
Answer: ⟦5|7⟧
Example 4 (add fractions with different denominators). Work out ⟦1|3⟧ + ⟦1|4⟧.
  1. The denominators are different (3 and 4), so find the LCM. Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. The LCM is 12.
  2. Change ⟦1|3⟧ to twelfths. 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so ⟦1|3⟧ = ⟦1 × 4|3 × 4⟧ = ⟦4|12⟧.
  3. Change ⟦1|4⟧ to twelfths. 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so ⟦1|4⟧ = ⟦3|12⟧.
  4. Add the numerators. ⟦4|12⟧ + ⟦3|12⟧ = ⟦7|12⟧.
  5. Simplify if possible. 7 and 12 have no common factor, so ⟦7|12⟧ is in simplest form.
Answer: ⟦7|12⟧
Example 5 (the answer is an improper fraction). Work out ⟦5|6⟧ + ⟦3|8⟧.
  1. Find the LCM of 6 and 8. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24. 24 is also a multiple of 6. The LCM is 24.
  2. Change both fractions to 24ths. ⟦5|6⟧ = ⟦5 × 4|6 × 4⟧ = ⟦20|24⟧ and ⟦3|8⟧ = ⟦3 × 3|8 × 3⟧ = ⟦9|24⟧.
  3. Add the numerators. ⟦20|24⟧ + ⟦9|24⟧ = ⟦29|24⟧.
  4. Write as a mixed number. 29 ÷ 24 = 1 remainder 5, so ⟦29|24⟧ = 1⟦5|24⟧.
Answer: ⟦29|24⟧ = 1⟦5|24⟧
Example 6 (subtract fractions). Work out (a) ⟦3|4⟧ − ⟦2|5⟧ and (b) ⟦7|8⟧ − ⟦1|6⟧.
  1. (a) LCM of 4 and 5. 20.
  2. (a) Convert. ⟦3|4⟧ = ⟦15|20⟧ and ⟦2|5⟧ = ⟦8|20⟧.
  3. (a) Subtract the numerators. 15 − 8 = 7, so ⟦15|20⟧ − ⟦8|20⟧ = ⟦7|20⟧.
  4. (b) LCM of 8 and 6. 24.
  5. (b) Convert. ⟦7|8⟧ = ⟦21|24⟧ and ⟦1|6⟧ = ⟦4|24⟧.
  6. (b) Subtract. 21 − 4 = 17, so the answer is ⟦17|24⟧.
Answer: (a) ⟦7|20⟧; (b) ⟦17|24⟧.
Example 7 (mixed numbers). Work out (a) 2⟦1|3⟧ + 1⟦3|4⟧ and (b) 3⟦1|4⟧ − 1⟦2|3⟧.
  1. (a) Add the whole numbers first. 2 + 1 = 3.
  2. (a) Add the fractions: LCM of 3 and 4 is 12. ⟦1|3⟧ + ⟦3|4⟧ = ⟦4|12⟧ + ⟦9|12⟧ = ⟦13|12⟧ = 1⟦1|12⟧.
  3. (a) Combine. 3 + 1⟦1|12⟧ = 4⟦1|12⟧.
  4. (b) Subtracting is easier if we change to improper fractions. 3⟦1|4⟧ = ⟦13|4⟧ (3 × 4 + 1 = 13) and 1⟦2|3⟧ = ⟦5|3⟧ (1 × 3 + 2 = 5).
  5. (b) Common denominator 12. ⟦13|4⟧ = ⟦39|12⟧ and ⟦5|3⟧ = ⟦20|12⟧.
  6. (b) Subtract. ⟦39|12⟧ − ⟦20|12⟧ = ⟦19|12⟧ = 1⟦7|12⟧.
Answer: (a) 4⟦1|12⟧; (b) 1⟦7|12⟧.
Example 8 (multiply fractions). Work out (a) ⟦2|3⟧ × ⟦4|5⟧, (b) ⟦3|8⟧ × ⟦4|9⟧, (c) ⟦3|5⟧ of 40.
  1. (a) Multiply the tops, then the bottoms. 2 × 4 = 8 and 3 × 5 = 15, so ⟦8|15⟧ (no common factor, so it is simplest).
  2. (b) Cancel first to keep the numbers small. 4 and 8 share the factor 4: 4 ÷ 4 = 1 and 8 ÷ 4 = 2. 3 and 9 share the factor 3: 3 ÷ 3 = 1 and 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
  3. (b) Multiply what is left. ⟦1|2⟧ × ⟦1|3⟧ = ⟦1|6⟧. (Without cancelling: ⟦12|72⟧ = ⟦1|6⟧, the same.)
  4. (c) “Of” means multiply. ⟦3|5⟧ × 40.
  5. (c) Divide 40 by the denominator, then multiply by the numerator. 40 ÷ 5 = 8 and 8 × 3 = 24.
Answer: (a) ⟦8|15⟧; (b) ⟦1|6⟧; (c) 24.
Example 9 (multiply mixed numbers). Work out 1⟦1|2⟧ × 2⟦2|3⟧.
  1. Change both to improper fractions first. 1⟦1|2⟧ = ⟦3|2⟧ and 2⟦2|3⟧ = ⟦8|3⟧.
  2. Multiply, cancelling where you can. ⟦3|2⟧ × ⟦8|3⟧: cancel 3 with 3, and 8 with 2 (8 ÷ 2 = 4). This leaves ⟦1|1⟧ × ⟦4|1⟧ = 4.
  3. Check with decimals. 1.5 × 2.667 ≈ 4 ✓.
Answer: 4
Example 10 (divide fractions: keep, change, flip). Work out (a) ⟦2|3⟧ ÷ ⟦4|5⟧ and (b) 3 ÷ ⟦1|4⟧.
  1. (a) Keep the first fraction. ⟦2|3⟧.
  2. (a) Change ÷ to ×, and flip the second fraction. ⟦2|3⟧ × ⟦5|4⟧.
  3. (a) Cancel and multiply. 2 and 4 share the factor 2: ⟦1|3⟧ × ⟦5|2⟧ = ⟦5|6⟧.
  4. (b) Think: how many quarters are in 3? Each whole has 4 quarters, so 3 wholes have 12.
  5. (b) Using the rule. 3 ÷ ⟦1|4⟧ = ⟦3|1⟧ × ⟦4|1⟧ = 12 ✓.
Answer: (a) ⟦5|6⟧; (b) 12.
Example 11 (divide mixed numbers). Work out 2⟦1|4⟧ ÷ 1⟦1|2⟧.
  1. Change to improper fractions. 2⟦1|4⟧ = ⟦9|4⟧ and 1⟦1|2⟧ = ⟦3|2⟧.
  2. Keep, change, flip. ⟦9|4⟧ × ⟦2|3⟧.
  3. Cancel. 9 and 3 share 3: 3 and 1. 2 and 4 share 2: 1 and 2. So ⟦3|2⟧ × ⟦1|1⟧ = ⟦3|2⟧.
  4. Write as a mixed number. ⟦3|2⟧ = 1⟦1|2⟧.
Answer: 1⟦1|2⟧
Example 12 (a word problem). A baker uses ⟦2|5⟧ of the flour for bread and ⟦1|4⟧ for cakes. What fraction of the flour is left?
  1. Add the fractions used. LCM of 5 and 4 is 20. ⟦2|5⟧ = ⟦8|20⟧ and ⟦1|4⟧ = ⟦5|20⟧. Total used = ⟦13|20⟧.
  2. Take it from the whole (1 = ⟦20|20⟧). ⟦20|20⟧ − ⟦13|20⟧ = ⟦7|20⟧.
  3. Check. ⟦13|20⟧ + ⟦7|20⟧ = ⟦20|20⟧ = 1 ✓.
Answer: ⟦7|20⟧ of the flour is left.
Common mistakes.
① Adding the denominators: ⟦1|2⟧ + ⟦1|3⟧ is not ⟦2|5⟧ (it is ⟦5|6⟧).
② Forgetting to change both the top and the bottom when making equivalent fractions.
③ Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing (flip only the second one).
④ Cancelling across a plus or minus sign (cancelling is only for multiplying).
⑤ Leaving the answer unsimplified.
Always check: does the answer make sense with a picture or a decimal?

3History & Facts

  • Ancient Egypt (about 1650 BCE): the Rhind Papyrus shows Egyptians writing most fractions as sums of unit fractions like ⟦1|2⟧ + ⟦1|4⟧.
  • India (about 500 CE): mathematicians wrote fractions with one number above the other. The fraction bar came later, from Arabic writers.
  • Latin “fractio”: the word fraction comes from the Latin for “broken”, because a fraction is a broken piece of a whole.
  • Music: note lengths are fractions: a half note, a quarter note and an eighth note. A bar in 4/4 time adds up to 1 whole.

5Real-World Applications

  • Cooking: recipes use ⟦1|2⟧ cup, ⟦3|4⟧ teaspoon; scaling a recipe means multiplying fractions.
  • Money and shopping: “⟦1|3⟧ off” in a sale, and sharing a bill equally.
  • Building and DIY: measurements such as 2⟦1|4⟧ metres of wood.
  • Music: notes are fractions of a bar.
  • Time: a quarter of an hour is ⟦1|4⟧ of 60 minutes = 15 minutes.

7Classwork & Homework

Classwork

  1. Write 12/18 in simplest form.
    AnswerThe HCF of 12 and 18 is 6, so ⟦12|18⟧ = ⟦2|3⟧.
  2. Work out ⟦3|5⟧ + ⟦1|10⟧.
    AnswerLCM 10: ⟦6|10⟧ + ⟦1|10⟧ = ⟦7|10⟧.
  3. Work out ⟦5|6⟧ − ⟦1|4⟧.
    AnswerLCM 12: ⟦10|12⟧ − ⟦3|12⟧ = ⟦7|12⟧.
  4. Work out ⟦2|5⟧ × ⟦15|16⟧.
    AnswerCancel 5 with 15 (1 and 3) and 2 with 16 (1 and 8): ⟦1|1⟧ × ⟦3|8⟧ = ⟦3|8⟧.

Homework

  1. Work out 1⟦2|3⟧ + 2⟦1|2⟧.
    AnswerWhole numbers 3. Fractions ⟦4|6⟧ + ⟦3|6⟧ = ⟦7|6⟧ = 1⟦1|6⟧. Total 4⟦1|6⟧.
  2. Work out ⟦3|4⟧ ÷ ⟦9|8⟧.
    AnswerKeep, change, flip: ⟦3|4⟧ × ⟦8|9⟧. Cancel 3 and 9 (1 and 3), and 8 and 4 (2 and 1): ⟦1|1⟧ × ⟦2|3⟧ = ⟦2|3⟧.
  3. Find ⟦5|8⟧ of 64 kg.
    Answer64 ÷ 8 = 8 and 8 × 5 = 40 kg.
  4. Sara spends ⟦1|3⟧ of her money on a book and ⟦1|4⟧ on lunch. What fraction is left?
    AnswerSpent ⟦4|12⟧ + ⟦3|12⟧ = ⟦7|12⟧. Left 1 − ⟦7|12⟧ = ⟦5|12⟧.

10Exam-style Questions

Marks: M = method, A = accuracy, R = reasoning. No calculator. Open “Mark scheme” only after trying.

Paper 1 style

Question 1 [4 marks] Work out, giving each answer as a fraction in its simplest form: (a) ⟦3|4⟧ + ⟦5|6⟧ [2] (b) ⟦7|8⟧ − ⟦1|3⟧ [2].
Mark scheme(a) M1 common denominator 12: ⟦9|12⟧ + ⟦10|12⟧; A1 ⟦19|12⟧ (= 1⟦7|12⟧). (b) M1 common denominator 24: ⟦21|24⟧ − ⟦8|24⟧; A1 ⟦13|24⟧.
Question 2 [4 marks] Work out (a) ⟦5|9⟧ × ⟦3|10⟧ [2] (b) 2⟦1|2⟧ ÷ ⟦5|6⟧ [2].
Mark scheme(a) M1 cancel or multiply: ⟦15|90⟧; A1 ⟦1|6⟧. (b) M1 ⟦5|2⟧ × ⟦6|5⟧; A1 3.
Question 3 [3 marks] A tank is ⟦2|5⟧ full. Then ⟦1|4⟧ of the whole tank is poured in. (a) What fraction of the tank is now full? [2] (b) What fraction is still empty? [1]
Mark scheme(a) M1 ⟦8|20⟧ + ⟦5|20⟧; A1 ⟦13|20⟧. (b) A1 ⟦7|20⟧.

Paper 2 style

Question 4 [6 marks] A recipe needs 1⟦3|4⟧ cups of flour for one cake. (a) How much flour is needed for 3 cakes? [2] (b) Jo has 10 cups of flour. How many whole cakes can she make? [3] (c) How much flour is left over? [1]
Mark scheme(a) M1 3 × ⟦7|4⟧; A1 ⟦21|4⟧ = 5⟦1|4⟧ cups. (b) M1 10 ÷ ⟦7|4⟧ = 10 × ⟦4|7⟧; A1 ⟦40|7⟧ = 5⟦5|7⟧; A1 5 cakes. (c) A1 10 − 5 × 1⟦3|4⟧ = 10 − 8.75 = 1⟦1|4⟧ cups.
