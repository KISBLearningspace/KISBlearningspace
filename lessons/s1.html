Sampling and Types of Data

Learning outcomes

  • Classify data as qualitative or quantitative, and quantitative data as discrete or continuous.

2Study Notes

Population. The whole group we want to know about (for example, all 1200 students at a school).
Sample. A smaller group taken from the population to give information about it.
Census. A survey of every member of the population.
Random sample. A sample chosen so that every member of the population has the same chance of being selected.
Bias. A sample is biased if some members of the population are more likely to be chosen than others, so the results are not representative.
Types of data.
• Qualitative (categorical): words or categories, such as eye colour or favourite sport.
• Quantitative (numerical): numbers.
  – Discrete: counted, can only take separate values (number of siblings: 0, 1, 2, …).
  – Continuous: measured, can take any value in an interval (height, time, mass).
Data such as shoe size are treated as discrete because only certain values are possible.
MethodHow it worksStrengthWeakness

Example 1 (classify). Classify each variable: (a) number of phones owned, (b) time to run 100 m, (c) favourite subject, (d) height of students in cm.
  1. Ask: is it a label or a number? Labels (categories) are qualitative. Numbers are quantitative.
  2. If it is a number, ask: can it be counted, or measured? Counted in whole steps (0, 1, 2, …) means discrete. Measured on a continuous scale means continuous.
  3. (a) A count, so quantitative discrete.
  4. (b) Time is measured and can take any value, so quantitative continuous.
  5. (c) A category, so qualitative.
  6. (d) Height is measured, so continuous, even if it is rounded to the nearest cm.
Answer: (a) quantitative discrete; (b) quantitative continuous; (c) qualitative; (d) quantitative continuous.
Example 2 (stratified). A school has 1200 students: 480 in Year 10, 360 in Year 11 and 360 in Year 12. A stratified sample of 60 is needed. How many from each year?
  1. Rule. Sample from a stratum = ⟦size of stratum|total population⟧ × sample size.
  2. Year 10. ⟦480|1200⟧ × 60 = 0.4 × 60 = 24.
  3. Year 11. ⟦360|1200⟧ × 60 = 0.3 × 60 = 18.
  4. Year 12. ⟦360|1200⟧ × 60 = 18.
  5. Check. 24 + 18 + 18 = 60 ✓
Answer: 24 from Year 10, 18 from Year 11, 18 from Year 12.
Example 3 (systematic). Take 40 students from a list of 1200 by systematic sampling.
  1. Find the interval. k = ⟦1200|40⟧ = 30.
  2. Pick a random start between 1 and k. Say 7.
  3. Add k each time. 7, 37, 67, 97, …
  4. Last member. The 40th number is 7 + 39 × 30 = 1177.
Answer: The sample is the students numbered 7, 37, 67, 97, …, 1177.
Example 4 (bias). A student surveys the first 30 people who walk into the school canteen at lunch to find the average hours of sleep. Evaluate the method.
  1. Name the method. Taking whoever is easiest to reach is a convenience sample.
  2. Look for who is missing. Only people who eat at school at that time are included. If Year 9 students arrive first, older students are under-represented.
  3. Decide about bias. The sample is not random, so it may not represent the school: it is biased.
  4. Improve it. Use stratified sampling by year group (or a random sample from the school list).
Answer: Convenience sample; biased. A stratified random sample would be better.
Example 5 (reliability and outliers). A data set contains a height of 382 cm. What should you do, and what makes data reliable?
  1. Spot the outlier. A value far from the others is an outlier.
  2. Investigate. A height of 382 cm is impossible for a person, so it is likely an error (perhaps 182 cm). Check the source.
  3. Decide. Correct or remove it only if you can justify it. A genuine unusual value should stay.
  4. Judge reliability. Data are more reliable if they come from a clear source, a large enough random sample, and consistent measurement. Missing data and very small samples reduce reliability.
Answer: Check outliers before removing them.
Think: ① A website poll says “90% of people prefer our product.” Who answered, and who did not? ② Why might a small random sample be better than a large biased one? ③ When is a census better than a sample?

3History & Facts

  • 1936 US election: a magazine surveyed millions of readers (a very large sample) and wrongly predicted the winner. The sample came from telephone and car owners, who were richer than the average voter. A smaller, better-chosen sample by Gallup gave the right result.
  • Pierre-Simon Laplace (about 1802): estimated the population of France from a sample of parishes, an early use of sampling.
  • Census: Thailand holds a national population and housing census about every ten years, a full count of the population.
  • Fact: pollsters now use quota and stratified methods with random digit dialling and weighting to reduce bias.

5Real-World Applications

  • Opinion polls and elections: stratified and quota samples by age, region and income.
  • Medicine: clinical trials sample patients at random to test a new drug.
  • Quality control: a factory tests every 50th item (systematic sampling) instead of every item.
  • Environmental science: counting trees or fish in sample plots to estimate a whole forest or lake.
  • Business: customer satisfaction surveys and market research.

7Classwork & Homework

Classwork

  1. Classify each variable: (a) number of goals in a match, (b) time to travel to school, (c) favourite colour, (d) mass of a rice bag.
    Answer(a) quantitative discrete, (b) quantitative continuous, (c) qualitative, (d) quantitative continuous.
  2. A school has 900 students: 360 in Grade 10, 300 in Grade 11 and 240 in Grade 12. Find the stratified sample of 90.
    AnswerGrade 10: ⟦360|900⟧ × 90 = 36. Grade 11: 30. Grade 12: 24. Total 90.
  3. A list has 600 names. Describe how to take a systematic sample of 30.
    Answerk = ⟦600|30⟧ = 20. Choose a random start from 1 to 20, then take every 20th name.
  4. A reporter asks the first 40 people leaving a gym whether they exercise regularly. Name the sampling method and give one reason it is biased.
    AnswerConvenience sampling. People at a gym are more likely to exercise, so the sample is not representative.

Homework

  1. Explain the difference between a census and a sample. Give one advantage of each.
    AnswerA census surveys every member (accurate but slow and costly). A sample surveys part of the population (cheaper and faster, but may be biased or less precise).
  2. A town has 5000 adults: 2000 aged 18 to 35, 1800 aged 36 to 55 and 1200 over 55. A quota sample of 50 is needed. How many from each group?
    Answer⟦2000|5000⟧ × 50 = 20; ⟦1800|5000⟧ × 50 = 18; ⟦1200|5000⟧ × 50 = 12.
  3. Give two reasons why a data value might be an outlier, and say what you should do with it.
    AnswerIt may be a measurement or recording error, or a genuine unusual value. Check the source; remove it only if it is an error.
  4. A website asks visitors to vote on a question. 85% vote yes. Explain why this is not a reliable estimate of public opinion.
    AnswerThe sample is self-selected (voluntary), only visitors respond, and people can vote more than once. It is not random.

10Exam-style Questions

Marks: M = method, A = accuracy, R = reasoning. Open “Mark scheme” only after trying.

Paper 1 style

Question 1 [6 marks] A school has 800 students: 320 in Year 10, 280 in Year 11 and 200 in Year 12. A stratified sample of 80 students is taken.
(a) Find the number of Year 11 students in the sample. [2]
(b) Find the numbers from Year 10 and Year 12. [2]
(c) The school also wants a systematic sample of 80 from an alphabetical list of all 800 students. Find the interval k and describe how to choose the sample. [2]
Mark scheme(a) M1 ⟦280|800⟧ × 80; A1 28.
(b) A1 32; A1 20 (check: 32 + 28 + 20 = 80).
(c) A1 k = ⟦800|80⟧ = 10; A1 choose a random start from 1 to 10, then take every 10th student.
Question 2 [5 marks] Classify each variable as qualitative, discrete or continuous: (a) the colour of a car [1] (b) the number of cars in a family [1] (c) the fuel used for a trip in litres [1]. (d) A researcher says “the survey of 2000 volunteers proves most people agree.” Give one reason this conclusion may be unreliable. [2]
Mark scheme(a) A1 qualitative.
(b) A1 discrete.
(c) A1 continuous.
(d) R1 volunteers are self-selected (not random); R1 so the sample is biased and a large size does not remove bias.

Paper 2 style

Question 3 [7 marks] A researcher wants to estimate the average number of hours teenagers spend on their phones. She asks 50 people at a shopping mall on a Saturday afternoon.
(a) Name the type of sampling used. [1]
(b) Give two reasons why this sample may be biased. [2]
(c) Suggest a better method, giving details. [2]
(d) State whether the number of hours is discrete or continuous. [1]
(e) One answer is 22 hours per day. Say what this value is called and what should be done. [1]
Mark scheme(a) A1 convenience sampling.
(b) R1 only people at the mall on Saturday (not all teenagers); R1 teenagers who like shopping or have free time may use phones more; (or other valid reasons).
(c) M1 stratified or simple random sample of teenagers from a school list across ages; A1 details (for example, sample sizes in proportion to the number of students in each year).
(d) A1 continuous.
(e) A1 outlier; check the value and remove only if it is an error (22 hours is not possible without sleep).
