Guiding questions

  • How can two variables be connected even when one does not cause the other?
  • What does a correlation coefficient of 0.9 or −0.3 tell us in words?
  • Can a data set have a clear pattern and still have r = 0?

Learning outcomes

  • Draw and describe a scatter diagram: direction, strength, linear or non-linear.
  • Define and interpret Pearson’s correlation coefficient r, using the GDC.
  • Identify outliers on a scatter diagram and explain their effect on r.
  • Explain why correlation does not prove causation.

2Study Notes

Bivariate data. Data in pairs (x, y), where two variables are measured for each individual (for example hours of study and test score). The independent (explanatory) variable x is the one we choose or that we think explains the other. The dependent (response) variable y is the one we think responds.
Scatter diagram. A graph of the points (x, y). It shows the direction (positive: y tends to increase as x increases; negative: y tends to decrease; none), the strength (how closely the points follow a pattern), and whether the pattern is linear. The mean point (x̄, ȳ) lies on the line of best fit.
Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient r. A number that measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship:
−1 ≤ r ≤ 1
r = 1: perfect positive linear correlation; r = −1: perfect negative; r = 0: no linear correlation. The sign gives the direction; |r| gives the strength. Use the GDC (two-variable statistics) to find r.
|r|Strength (a guide)
0 to 0.25very weak or none
0.25 to 0.5weak
0.5 to 0.75moderate
0.75 to 0.9strong
0.9 to 1very strong
Example 1 (describe). Hours of study x and test score y for seven students: (1, 52), (2, 55), (3, 61), (4, 60), (5, 68), (6, 72), (7, 75). Describe the relationship.
  1. Mean point. x̄ = ⟦1 + 2 + … + 7|7⟧ = 4 and ȳ = ⟦443|7⟧ ≈ 63.3.
  2. Plot or imagine the scatter diagram. The points rise from left to right in a nearly straight pattern.
  3. Find r with the GDC. Enter x in List 1 and y in List 2, then choose two-variable statistics (linear regression): r ≈ 0.983.
  4. Describe in words. Direction: positive (r > 0). Strength: 0.983 is close to 1, so very strong. Form: linear.
Answer: Strong (very strong) positive linear correlation, r ≈ 0.983.
Example 2 (interpret r). Describe the correlation for (a) r = −0.82, (b) r = 0.12, (c) r = 0.6.
  1. Sign gives the direction. Negative means y tends to decrease as x increases.
  2. Size gives the strength. Compare |r| with the guide: below 0.25 very weak, 0.25–0.5 weak, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.9 strong, above 0.9 very strong.
  3. (a) −0.82 has size 0.82: strong negative.
  4. (b) 0.12 is below 0.25: very weak, almost no linear correlation.
  5. (c) 0.6 is between 0.5 and 0.75: moderate positive.
Answer: (a) strong negative; (b) very weak; (c) moderate positive.
Example 3 (r = 0 does not mean no relationship). The points (−3, 9), (−2, 4), (−1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9) lie on y = x². Find r and comment.
  1. Plot the points. They form a U-shaped parabola, perfectly arranged.
  2. Calculate r. Enter the data in the GDC: r = 0, because for every positive x there is an equal y for −x, so there is no overall upward or downward trend.
  3. Interpret. r measures only linear relationships. Here the relationship is perfect, but not linear.
Answer: r = 0, yet there is a perfect quadratic relationship. Always look at the scatter diagram first.
Example 4 (an outlier). Add the point (8, 30) to the data of Example 1. What happens to r?
  1. Spot the point. The other points have y between 52 and 75, so (8, 30) is far below the pattern.
  2. Recalculate r on the GDC with 8 points. r ≈ −0.03.
  3. Compare. r fell from 0.983 to about −0.03: a strong correlation became almost none.
  4. Decide what to do. Check whether the point is an error before removing it. Never remove it just because it spoils the fit.
Answer: One outlier can change r a great deal.
Correlation does not mean causation. A strong correlation between x and y does not prove that x causes y. There may be:
• a lurking (confounding) variable that affects both (hot weather increases both ice-cream sales and drowning accidents),
• a coincidence, or
• reverse causation.
Causation needs a controlled experiment or strong evidence of a mechanism.
Example 5 (extension: r²). Find r² for Example 1 and interpret it.
  1. Square r. r² = 0.983² ≈ 0.966.
  2. Convert to a percentage. 0.966 = 96.6%.
  3. Interpret. r² is the proportion of the variation in y that is explained by its linear relationship with x.
Answer: About 96.6% of the variation in test scores is explained by the hours studied.
Think: ① Why does r not change if you change the units (for example centimetres to metres)? ② If r = 0.95 between the number of firefighters and the damage at a fire, does adding firefighters cause damage? ③ How can an outlier make a strong relationship look weak (or the reverse)?

3History & Facts

  • Francis Galton (1880s): measured heights of parents and children and noticed “regression towards the mean”, which started the study of correlation.
  • Karl Pearson (1895): gave the product-moment formula for the correlation coefficient r.
  • Spurious correlations: the number of films Nicolas Cage appeared in correlates with swimming-pool drownings in the US (a coincidence).
  • Fact: Galton found that tall parents tended to have children closer to average height, not even taller.

5Real-World Applications

  • Medicine: links between smoking and lung disease, or between exercise and blood pressure, are first found as correlations.
  • Economics: advertising spend and sales, or education and income.
  • Environmental science: temperature and the growth of coral bleaching.
  • Sport: training hours and race times.
  • Social media and technology: screen time and sleep, studied with scatter diagrams.

6IB Links: TOK, International-mindedness & ATL

  • TOK: “Correlation does not imply causation.” How do we ever establish a cause? Is “cause” a statistical or a scientific idea?
  • International-mindedness: public health links (such as smoking or air pollution and disease) shaped laws in many countries. How strong must evidence be before action?
  • ATL: thinking (look for lurking variables), research (find real bivariate data from reliable sources).
  • IA ideas: investigate whether study hours or sleep hours correlate with test results · explore the relation between GDP and life expectancy in several countries · compare correlation in two groups.

7Classwork & Homework

Classwork

  1. Describe the correlation if r = −0.82 and if r = 0.12.
    Answer
    r = −0.82: strong negative linear correlation
    r = 0.12: very weak, almost no linear correlation
  2. For the data (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 7), (5, 8), (6, 9), find the mean point.
    Answer
    x̄ = 3.5
    ȳ = 6
    The mean point is (3.5, 6)
  3. The GDC gives r = 0.949 for the data in CW 2. Describe the correlation.
    AnswerStrong (very strong) positive linear correlation.
  4. Can r be 1.3? Explain.
    AnswerNo. r always satisfies −1 ≤ r ≤ 1.

Homework

  1. A study finds r = 0.8 between the number of ice creams sold and the number of drowning accidents. Does eating ice cream cause drowning? Explain.
    Answer
    No
    A lurking variable (hot weather) increases both
    Correlation does not prove causation
  2. The points (−3, 9), (−2, 4), (−1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9) lie on y = x². Why can r be 0?
    Answer
    r measures linear correlation only
    the relationship is non-linear (a parabola)
  3. One point is far from the rest of a scatter diagram. Give two things you should do before deciding to remove it.
    Answer
    Check whether it is a recording or measurement error
    compare r with and without it
    consider whether it is a genuine value
  4. Does r change if all x-values are converted from centimetres to metres? Explain.
    AnswerNo. r is not affected by changes of units (a linear change of scale), because it measures the pattern, not the size.
